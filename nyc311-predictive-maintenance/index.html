Issue 01 · NYC 311 · Brooklyn

Which building will complain next?

Two years of Brooklyn housing complaints, 7,418 buildings arranged by how they break, and one chain of cause and effect that turned out to be something else.

Data: NYC Open Data, 311 Service Requests (HPD), Nov 2024 – Oct 2026 · Scroll to read, or jump to the explorer

New York publishes every housing complaint its residents file through 311: no heat, a leak, mice, a door that won’t lock. For anyone who looks after buildings, it is the closest public thing to a ticket log.

I started with a simple question. Can old tickets predict the next one? The answer is yes, with a qualification. On the way there, the most convincing pattern in the data turned out to answer a different question altogether.

1 · The mess

From complaints to buildings

The raw file holds 478,559 complaints. Most of the early work is deciding what counts as one problem.

When the heating fails, ten tenants call. That is one boiler, not ten problems. So complaints from the same building, in the same category, on the same day become one incident.

29,306 buildings had at least one incident. Most had very few: 8,909 had exactly one. A building with three complaints has no meaningful “profile”, so the graph keeps buildings with ten or more.

That leaves 7,418 buildings: a quarter of the buildings, but 82% of all incidents.

Each dot on the right is one of them. Each line links a building to the five buildings that break most like it.

2 · Personalities

Every building breaks in its own way

Describe each building by how it breaks: how often, which categories, how often the same problem comes back within 30 days, how long repairs take, how much of it happens in winter.

Then let a clustering algorithm sort them. Nobody tells it what a “heat building” is. It finds ten groups anyway.

The largest group, 1,408 buildings, is mostly heat and hot water. Repairs close fast, in a median of 2.4 days.

And then 60% of their incidents happen again within a month. Fast repairs and the same failure again is what a boiler that needs replacing looks like in a ticket log.

1,313 buildings file a bit of everything, at a median 3.5 incidents a month, several times the rate of any other group.

No single trade fixes these. They need someone to walk through the building.

And then a small group of 192 where the median complaint stays open for 46 days.

Their complaints are ordinary. The response time is what sets them apart.

3 · Geography

Trouble has an address

The same buildings, now placed where they stand.

An anomaly is a week when a building files far more than its own history and the season would predict. Two different detectors have to agree: a statistical test and an isolation forest. Over two years, 630 building-weeks pass both.

143 of these buildings had an anomaly in the last eight weeks. More than a third of them sit in five ZIP codes: Bedford-Stuyvesant, Ocean Hill, Brownsville, Bushwick and Flatbush.

The typical spike is a quiet building that suddenly files 10–25 issues across five or six categories in a single week. It looks less like everything breaking at once and more like someone finally writing it all down: an inspection, a tenants’ association, a new owner.

4 · The twist

The chain that wasn’t

Anyone who maintains buildings knows this story: first a leak, then mold, then the ceiling needs paint.

The data seems to agree. Count how often problem B follows problem A within 30 days in the same building, compare it with the borough average, and you get 680 “chains”, nearly everything linked to nearly everything.

The water family glows brightest. Mold is followed by a damp spot 4.3× more often than average. A heavy leak is followed by ceiling paint 3.6× more often.

That looks like a good basis for prediction.

The catch is the comparison. Buildings that leak are buildings that have everything. Compare each building with its own normal rate instead, and the water chain drops to 1.2–1.4×, barely above chance.

The pattern is real. It just answers a different question: it tells you which buildings are in trouble, not what happens next in them.

Only 23 chains survive. The strongest are almost boringly physical: a power outage followed by no lighting, a cooking-gas shut-off followed by work on the range, a heating failure followed by work on the radiator.

Cause and effect turns out to be mostly about electricity and gas.

5 · Prediction

Before it happens

So, back to the first question. A model trained on monthly snapshots from May 2025 to March 2026 ranks every building–category pair by the chance of at least one incident in the next 30 days.

It was tested on months it had never seen, May to September 2026. Its top 1,000 flags each month were right 58% of the time. The obvious rule, “whatever happened recently will happen again”, managed 46%.

The more useful list is one a hotspot map cannot produce: 205 buildings flagged for a category they have not reported for at least 90 days.

On the test months, lists like this were about 10× better than picking at random. Not a crystal ball. A shortlist for someone with a clipboard.

How this was made

Data

311 Service Requests from NYC Open Data (dataset erm2-nwe9), filtered to agency HPD, borough Brooklyn, created 1 November 2024 – 27 October 2026. Fields used: building ID (BBL), address and coordinates, complaint category and sub-category, dates opened and closed, status. Tenant, unit and free-text resolution were not used.

Methods

  • Incidents: complaints merged by building × category × day. One placeholder building ID (0000000000) was excluded.
  • Segments: per-building profile (volume, category mix, 30-day repeat rate, median days to close, open share, heat-season share, reports per incident) → PCA → KMeans with k = 10 chosen by silhouette. The silhouette is low (0.13): buildings form a continuum, so the segments are useful labels, not sharp types. The similarity layout is UMAP; lines are 5 nearest neighbours.
  • Anomalies: expected weekly count = the building’s share of the trailing 26 weeks × the borough total that week (so seasonality is built in). Flagged when a Poisson tail test (p < 10⁻⁴, ≥ 4 incidents) and an Isolation Forest agree.
  • Chains: B within 1–30 days after A in the same building. Raw lift compares with a borough-wide rate; timing lift compares with each building’s own rate of B. Communities: Louvain.
  • Prediction: gradient-boosted trees on recency and frequency features, monthly snapshots, strict time split. ROC-AUC 0.82 against 0.74 for the recency rule.

Caveats

311 is tenant-reported and regulator-facing, not an operator’s ticket log. Duplicates and inspection sweeps inflate spikes. The test period covers one summer only, so heat predictions need a winter check. Flags and risk scores are model outputs on public data, not statements about the condition of any building.
